\title{Adaptive Incremental Line Search: A Dynamic Corridor-Based Optimization Framework for Grid-Based Pathfinding in Robotics and Autonomous Systems}

\author{\IEEEauthorblockN{Amr Elshahed\textsuperscript{1}, Majid Khan Bin Majahar Ali\textsuperscript{2,*}, Farah Aini Binti Abdullah\textsuperscript{1}}
\IEEEauthorblockA{\textsuperscript{1}School of Computer Sciences, Universiti Sains Malaysia, 11800 USM, Penang, Malaysia\\

\begin{abstract}
Computing shortest paths on grid maps is a core task in robotics, autonomous navigation, and game AI.
Corridor-based methods restrict graph search to a band around a reference trajectory, but existing approaches---including our own Incremental Line Search (ILS)---use a \emph{uniform} corridor width.
A uniform width must be set wide enough for the densest segment of the reference line, which wastes search space in open segments and yields redundant expansions when obstacle topology is heterogeneous.
We present the \emph{Adaptive Incremental Line Search} (AILS), a \textbf{topologically robust} corridor-based framework that overcomes this limitation.
At every cell on a Bresenham reference line, a local obstacle-density estimate $\sigma(p)$---computed in $O(1)$ via integral images---determines the corridor radius through the closed-form mapping $r(p) = r_{\min} + \lfloor (r_{\max} - r_{\min}) \cdot \sigma(p)^{\alpha} \rfloor$, producing a non-convex, variable-width band that is narrow in open passages and widens only where obstacles demand it.
Any admissible graph-search algorithm (A*, Dijkstra, BFS) can be executed within this restricted search space without modification; a fallback expansion mechanism preserves completeness (proved by induction on the expansion index over the finite vertex set).
We evaluate AILS on synthetic 8-connected grids from $50{\times}50$ to $500{\times}500$ with five obstacle topologies (Random, Clustered, Maze, Room, Open) and densities of 10--40\%.
On $200{\times}200$ grids at 25\% density, AILS-Base explores 56.0\% fewer nodes than A* and AILS-Adaptive explores 51.4\% fewer nodes; both reductions are statistically significant (paired $t$-tests, $p < 0.001$; $t = 12.39$ and $t = 11.26$; Cohen's $d = 0.82$ and $0.76$, respectively).
Node reduction scales with grid size---from 5.1\% on $50{\times}50$ to 76.8\% on $500{\times}500$---and AILS-Adaptive becomes time-competitive with A* at $300{\times}300$ and above, reducing mean execution time by 6.3\% while exploring 65.5\% fewer nodes on $300{\times}300$ grids.
To validate scalability to real-world problem sizes, we additionally benchmark AILS on large-scale grids from $1{,}000{\times}1{,}000$ to $10{,}000{\times}10{,}000$ (up to $10^8$ cells) against A* and Jump Point Search (JPS), with 630 trials across clustered, mixed, and random obstacle patterns at 20--30\% density.
At this scale, AILS achieves $39.9{\times}$ speedup over A* with 97.2\% node reduction on $10{,}000{\times}10{,}000$ grids ($p < 0.001$; Cohen's $d = 1.65$, large effect), while exploring only 0.11\% of the grid.
Power-law fitting reveals AILS scales as $O(n^{0.51})$---effectively \emph{sublinear}---compared to $O(n^{0.85})$ for both A* and JPS, demonstrating that the corridor efficiency improves as grid size increases.
The principal advantage over ILS is not raw speed on uniform-density grids---where ILS's simpler mechanism already excels---but \emph{robustness across diverse obstacle topologies}: AILS maintains efficiency in environments with spatially varying density, where a global corridor width would either over-expand in open regions or under-expand near obstacles.
AILS is most effective in random and open layouts at 10--25\% density, conditions common in outdoor robotics and warehouse navigation.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Pathfinding on grid-based maps is a core problem in robotics~\cite{lavalle2006planning,siciliano2016springer}, autonomous vehicles~\cite{paden2016survey,gonzalez2015review}, UAV navigation~\cite{goerzen2010survey,majeed2024uav}, and multi-agent coordination~\cite{stern2019mapf}. The central challenge is to compute optimal or near-optimal paths while keeping computational cost low enough for real-time operation~\cite{bento2021autonomous}.

\subsection{Motivation and Problem Statement}

A*~\cite{hart1968formal} and Dijkstra's algorithm~\cite{dijkstra1959note} guarantee shortest paths, but both expand many nodes that lie far from the eventual solution, especially when the optimal path is roughly direct.
A* mitigates this through heuristic guidance, yet on large grids its explored-node count still grows with the square of the grid dimension~\cite{sturtevant2012benchmarks}.
Jump Point Search (JPS)~\cite{harabor2011online} dramatically reduces expansions on uniform-cost grids by pruning symmetric sub-paths, but it does not apply to weighted or non-uniform grids.
Hierarchical methods such as HPA*~\cite{botea2004near} and Contraction Hierarchies~\cite{geisberger2008contraction} precompute abstractions for fast queries, yet the preprocessing cost limits their use when maps change at runtime.
Incremental planners such as D*~Lite~\cite{koenig2002d} and LPA*~\cite{koenig2004lifelong} reuse previous search trees after local map changes, but they do not reduce the initial search space.

These limitations are acutely felt in real-time robotics~\cite{liu2024hybrid,pmc2025review}, resource-constrained embedded platforms~\cite{chen2024safety}, and dynamic environments requiring frequent replanning~\cite{koenig2004lifelong,nair2024robust}.
A complementary strategy is to \emph{restrict the search space} to a corridor around a reference trajectory, so that the planner examines only a small fraction of the grid.
Existing corridor-based methods, however, typically use a fixed corridor width or expand the entire corridor uniformly when the initial band is insufficient (see Section~\ref{sec:related} for a detailed comparison).

\subsection{Relationship to ILS and Novelty of This Work}
\label{sec:ils_novelty}

A preliminary version of corridor-based pathfinding was introduced by the present authors as the \emph{Incremental Line Search} (ILS)~\cite{elshahed2025ils}, published in IEEE Access.
ILS constructs a \emph{uniform-width} corridor of fixed radius~$r$ around the Bresenham reference line; the same radius is applied at every cell regardless of the local obstacle configuration.
When the corridor is too narrow to contain a feasible path, ILS widens it by a constant increment~$\Delta r$ uniformly along the entire line.
The corridor width can be adjusted manually between queries, but no mechanism exists for automatic, spatially varying adaptation within a single query.

The present work---\emph{Adaptive} ILS (AILS)---addresses this fundamental limitation by introducing \textbf{per-point local density estimation} via integral images.
For each cell~$p$ on the Bresenham line, the local obstacle density~$\sigma(p)$ is computed in $O(1)$ time using a precomputed summed-area table, and the corridor radius is set by the closed-form mapping
\begin{equation}
    r(p) = r_{\min} + \bigl\lfloor (r_{\max} - r_{\min}) \cdot \sigma(p)^{\alpha} \bigr\rfloor.
    \label{eq:radius_intro}
\end{equation}
Because $r(p)$ varies independently at every reference-line cell, the resulting corridor is a union of Chebyshev balls of different radii---a \emph{non-convex} region that can be narrow in open areas and wide only where obstacles are present.
ILS, by contrast, produces only rectangular (convex) corridors of uniform width and cannot represent such spatially heterogeneous bands.

\paragraph{Why a more complex adaptive mechanism?}
ILS achieves impressive node reductions (up to 87\% on uniform-density random grids~\cite{elshahed2025ils}) precisely because a single global radius~$r$ is well-matched to environments where obstacle density is spatially homogeneous.
However, in \emph{topologically heterogeneous} environments---mazes with alternating narrow corridors and open chambers, room layouts with doorways, or maps with clustered obstacle groups---the global radius~$r$ must be set to the maximum needed anywhere along the reference line.
This forces ILS to over-expand in every open segment, leading to redundant node expansions that erode its advantage.
AILS trades the simplicity of a single radius for \emph{topological robustness}: the per-cell radius $r(p)$ stays narrow ($r_{\min}$) wherever the local neighbourhood is clear and widens only where obstacles are present, keeping the corridor area proportional to the \emph{weighted average} density rather than the \emph{peak} density.
On uniform random grids, where ILS already excels, AILS's adaptive mechanism adds overhead that slightly reduces the raw node-reduction percentage; on heterogeneous layouts, it prevents the systematic over-expansion that degrades ILS.
Section~\ref{sec:results} confirms this trade-off empirically.

\subsection{Contributions}

Building on the ILS framework~\cite{elshahed2025ils}, this paper introduces the Adaptive Incremental Line Search (AILS), a corridor-based pathfinding framework whose corridor width varies \emph{per cell} along the reference line according to local obstacle density.
The specific contributions beyond ILS are as follows.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Per-point adaptive corridor construction.}
    We propose a corridor-building procedure in which the radius at each cell on a Bresenham reference line is a closed-form function of the local obstacle density, estimated via integral images in $O(1)$ per query.
    This produces a variable-width band that is narrow in open regions and wide only where obstacles are present.

    \item \textbf{Algorithm-agnostic integration.}
    The corridor is used solely to filter the neighbor set of the base search algorithm; consequently, any admissible graph search (A*, Dijkstra, BFS) can be plugged in without modification. A fallback expansion mechanism progressively widens the corridor until a path is found or the entire grid has been explored, preserving the completeness of the base algorithm.

    \item \textbf{Multi-strategy corridor selection.}
    AILS provides three corridor strategies---fixed-width, density-adaptive, and gradient-enhanced---and selects among them based on a quick density scan of the reference line. The gradient-enhanced variant uses density gradients for predictive (lookahead) expansion before dense regions are reached.

    \item \textbf{Comprehensive empirical evaluation across scales.}
    We test AILS on over 9{,}000 synthetic grid configurations (sizes $50{\times}50$ to $500{\times}500$, five obstacle patterns, densities 10--40\%) with paired $t$-tests, effect-size analysis, and scalability curves.
    We additionally benchmark AILS on large-scale grids ($1{,}000{\times}1{,}000$ to $10{,}000{\times}10{,}000$, 630 trials) against A* and JPS, demonstrating $39.9{\times}$ speedup at the largest scale and sublinear $O(n^{0.51})$ time scaling. We also compare against recent corridor and coverage-planning work~\cite{lee2025mcpp,huang2024corridor}.

    \item \textbf{Formal analysis of correctness and complexity.}
    We state conditions under which AILS inherits the completeness of the base algorithm and provide worst-case time and space complexity bounds for corridor construction and search.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

We organize prior work into five areas and, for each, identify the specific limitation that AILS addresses.

\subsection{Classical and Incremental Pathfinding}

Dijkstra's algorithm~\cite{dijkstra1959note} finds shortest paths through exhaustive expansion with $O((|V|+|E|)\log |V|)$ time.
A*~\cite{hart1968formal} reduces expansion by adding an admissible heuristic~$h(n)$; on 8-connected grids the octile distance is a standard choice~\cite{sturtevant2012benchmarks}.
Bidirectional A*~\cite{russell2016artificial} runs two searches that meet in the middle, lowering the effective branching depth.
None of these methods restrict the search space \emph{a priori}; they differ only in the order of expansion.

For environments that change between queries, D*~Lite~\cite{koenig2002d} and LPA*~\cite{koenig2004lifelong} \emph{incrementally} update a previous search tree rather than replanning from scratch.
Their ``adaptiveness'' refers to reacting to \emph{map changes between successive queries}, not to adjusting the search region within a single query.
By contrast, AILS adapts the \emph{search region} (corridor width) within a single query based on obstacle density; it does not reuse information across successive queries.
The two ideas are complementary: an incremental planner could, in principle, be run inside an AILS corridor, although we do not explore this combination here.

Liu et al.~\cite{liu2024hybrid} combined A* with a dynamic-window approach for mobile-robot navigation, and recent surveys~\cite{pmc2025review,bento2021autonomous} catalogue the growing demand for real-time planners on resource-constrained platforms~\cite{chen2024safety}.

\subsection{Search-Space Reduction Techniques}

Jump Point Search (JPS)~\cite{harabor2011online} prunes symmetric paths on \emph{uniform-cost} grids, achieving 10--100$\times$ speed-ups over A* by expanding only ``jump points.''
JPS does not generalize to weighted grids.

Hierarchical methods decompose the map into coarser abstractions.
HPA*~\cite{botea2004near} clusters the grid and precomputes intra-cluster paths; Contraction Hierarchies~\cite{geisberger2008contraction} add shortcut edges during an offline contraction phase.
Both achieve fast queries but require preprocessing that must be repeated when the map changes.

Theta*~\cite{nash2007theta} allows line-of-sight shortcuts between non-adjacent nodes, producing any-angle paths that can be shorter than grid-constrained solutions.
This idea of checking line-of-sight between grid cells is conceptually related to AILS's use of a Bresenham reference line; however, Theta* uses line-of-sight during search as a \emph{path-smoothing} step, whereas AILS uses the Bresenham line \emph{before} search to define the corridor.

\textbf{Position of AILS.}
AILS reduces the search space like hierarchical methods but requires \emph{no preprocessing or precomputed hierarchy}; it works on \emph{any} edge-weighted grid (unlike JPS); and it restricts the search region (unlike incremental planners, which restrict replanning effort).

\subsection{Corridor and Channel-Based Methods}

Corridor-based planning restricts search to a band around a reference trajectory.
Voronoi-based planners~\cite{aurenhammer1991voronoi} define maximally-safe corridors but require global diagram construction.
Sampling-based planners (PRM~\cite{kavraki1996probabilistic}, RRT/RRT*~\cite{lavalle1998rapidly,karaman2011sampling}) generate implicit corridors in high-dimensional spaces; they are less common for 2-D grid search.

Recent work targets real-time corridor generation.
The Bubble Planner~\cite{chen2024bubble} creates receding convex corridors for high-speed quadrotor flight, adjusting boundaries from sensor data.
Wu et al.~\cite{wu2023corridor} build corridors for quadrotor teams in cluttered environments.
Zhou et al.~\cite{zhou2024local} use a local-density measure for mobile-robot planning, but their density is computed over fixed-size regions and feeds a potential-field controller rather than a corridor for graph search.
Huang et al.~\cite{huang2024corridor} and Cohen et al.~\cite{cohen2023corridor} define corridors for multi-agent coordination, and Wang et al.~\cite{wang2025adaptive} analyse adaptive corridors for multi-robot planning.

\textbf{ILS and its limitations.}
Our earlier work, the Incremental Line Search (ILS)~\cite{elshahed2025ils}, introduced the idea of constructing a corridor along a Bresenham reference line and incrementally widening it upon search failure.
ILS employs a \emph{uniform} corridor radius~$r$ that is the same at every reference-line cell; the radius can be adjusted manually between queries but does not vary spatially within a single query.
Consequently, the ILS corridor is always a convex rectangular band: it must be set wide enough to accommodate the densest segment of the reference line, which wastes search space in open segments.
Furthermore, ILS provides no mechanism for anticipating density transitions (no gradient lookahead) and uses global rather than local expansion when the corridor is insufficient.

\textbf{Key gap.}
Existing corridor methods---including ILS---use either a \emph{fixed} width or a \emph{region-level} adaptive width.
AILS is, to our knowledge, the first to compute a \emph{per-cell} corridor radius from a closed-form density function along a reference line, mapping local occupancy information into a variable radius $r(p) = r_{\min} + \lfloor (r_{\max} - r_{\min}) \cdot \sigma(p)^{\alpha} \rfloor$ via integral-image queries.
This produces a continuously varying, \emph{non-convex} corridor shape that ILS and other fixed-width methods cannot represent (see Section~\ref{sec:ils_novelty} and Table~\ref{tab:ils_vs_ails}).

\subsection{Occupancy-Based Density Estimation}

Moravec~\cite{moravec1988sensor} introduced certainty grids for probabilistic occupancy mapping; OctoMap~\cite{hornung2013octomap} extends this idea to efficient 3-D octree representations.
AILS uses a simpler model: deterministic occupancy on a known grid, with local density computed via integral images (summed-area tables) for $O(1)$ window queries.
This avoids maintaining a full probabilistic map and is sufficient for the static-grid setting we study.

\subsection{Multi-Agent Pathfinding, Coverage Planning, and Learning-Based Methods}

Multi-agent pathfinding (MAPF) seeks collision-free paths for multiple agents simultaneously.
Conflict-Based Search (CBS)~\cite{sharon2015conflict} provides optimal MAPF solutions; EECBS~\cite{li2021eecbs} trades optimality for speed; Stern et al.~\cite{stern2019mapf} define standard MAPF benchmarks.
Galceran and Carreras~\cite{galceran2013survey} survey coverage path planning (CPP) for robotics.
Lee and Lee~\cite{lee2025mcpp} recently proposed MCPP-GAK for multi-agent coverage using graph-adapted K-means clustering.
AILS solves a different problem---single-agent point-to-point shortest paths---but faster single-agent planning could accelerate the internal pathfinding calls within MAPF or CPP frameworks.

Deep reinforcement learning has been applied to path planning~\cite{zhang2023deep}; these methods learn policies rather than computing explicit corridors and are outside the scope of our comparison.

(iii)~most corridor methods are coupled to a specific search algorithm.

\section{Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:problem}

We first fix notation that will be used throughout the paper, then state the optimization objective that AILS targets.

\subsection{Graph Representation}

The environment is modelled as an 8-connected grid graph $G = (V, E, w)$, where:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $V = \{v_{x,y} : 0 \leq x < H,\; 0 \leq y < W_g\}$ is the vertex set ($H$ rows, $W_g$ columns), with $x$ indexing rows and $y$ indexing columns;
    \item $E \subseteq V \times V$ contains edges between each cell and its (up to) eight neighbours;
    \item $w : E \to \mathbb{R}^{+}$ assigns edge costs: $w(e) = 1$ for cardinal moves, $w(e) = \sqrt{2}$ for diagonal moves.
\end{itemize}

\noindent
Each vertex carries a binary occupancy label:
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{occ}(v) = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } v \text{ is blocked (obstacle),} \\ 0 & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases}
\end{equation}
The obstacle set is $\mathcal{O} = \{v \in V : \mathrm{occ}(v) = 1\}$.
A vertex~$v$ is \emph{traversable} iff $\mathrm{occ}(v) = 0$.

\paragraph{Notational conventions.}
Throughout the paper, we use the coordinate pair $(x, y)$ to refer to the row and column of a grid cell, respectively.
We reserve $w$ for the edge-weight function.
The density-estimation \emph{window half-size} is denoted~$\omega$ (see Section~\ref{sec:method}), and the full window side length is $2\omega+1$.
Corridor radii are written $r_{\min}$, $r_{\max}$, and $r(p)$ (per-point radius).
The density at a point~$p$ is $\sigma(p) \in [0,1]$.

\subsection{Pathfinding Objective}

Given a traversable start vertex $s \in V \setminus \mathcal{O}$ and goal vertex $g \in V \setminus \mathcal{O}$, an \emph{optimal path} is a sequence $\pi^{*} = (v_0, v_1, \ldots, v_k)$ with $v_0 = s$, $v_k = g$, every $v_i$ traversable, and consecutive vertices adjacent, that minimises total cost:
\begin{equation}
    \pi^{*} = \arg\min_{\pi \in \Pi(s,g)} \sum_{i=0}^{|\pi|-1} w(v_i, v_{i+1})
\end{equation}
where $\Pi(s,g)$ is the set of all valid (obstacle-free) paths from $s$ to $g$.

\subsection{Corridor-Constrained Search}

An \emph{adaptive corridor} $C_a \subseteq V$ is a subset of vertices used to restrict the search.
We say $C_a$ \emph{covers} a path $\pi$ iff every vertex of $\pi$ lies in $C_a$.
The corridor-constrained shortest path is:
\begin{equation}
    \pi^{*}_C = \arg\min_{\pi \in \Pi_C(s,g)} \sum_{i=0}^{|\pi|-1} w(v_i, v_{i+1}), \quad
    \Pi_C(s,g) = \{\pi \in \Pi(s,g) : \forall v \in \pi,\; v \in C_a\}.
\end{equation}
If $C_a$ covers at least one globally optimal path~$\pi^{*}$, then $\pi^{*}_C = \pi^{*}$ (the corridor search returns the global optimum).
Otherwise, $\pi^{*}_C$ is the best path \emph{within} the corridor, and its cost satisfies $\mathrm{cost}(\pi^{*}_C) \geq \mathrm{cost}(\pi^{*})$.

\subsection{Design Objectives}

AILS aims to construct a small corridor $C_a$ that, in practice, covers an optimal path.
The three design goals are:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Completeness (via fallback).}
    If a path exists in $G$ and no path is found inside the initial corridor, AILS iteratively expands $C_a$.
    In the worst case $C_a$ grows to $V$, so the procedure is complete whenever the base search algorithm is complete (see Theorem~\ref{thm:completeness}).

    \item \textbf{Near-optimality.}
    When the corridor covers an optimal path, the returned solution is globally optimal.
    When it does not (before expansion), the solution is the shortest corridor-constrained path; empirically, this is within a few percent of the optimum on the grid sizes and densities we test (Section~\ref{sec:results}).

    \item \textbf{Efficiency.}
    In typical scenarios $|C_a| \ll |V|$, so the constrained search examines far fewer nodes than an unconstrained search.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Proposed Method: Adaptive Incremental Line Search}
\label{sec:method}

This section describes the four stages of AILS---reference-line generation, per-point density estimation, adaptive corridor construction, and corridor-constrained search with fallback---followed by the multi-strategy selection rule and formal complexity analysis.
All notation follows Section~\ref{sec:problem}.

\subsection{Algorithm Overview}

AILS restricts a standard graph search to a \emph{variable-width corridor} centred on a Bresenham line from start~$s$ to goal~$g$.
The corridor radius at each reference-line cell is a closed-form function of the local obstacle density, so the band is narrow in open regions and wide near obstacles.
If the constrained search fails to find a path, AILS widens the corridor incrementally until a solution is found or the full grid has been explored.

\subsection{Stage 1: Bresenham Reference Line}
\label{sec:bresenham}

The corridor is centred on a Bresenham line~\cite{bresenham1965algorithm} $L = \mathrm{Bresenham}(s, g)$, the standard integer-arithmetic rasterisation of the segment from~$s$ to~$g$:
\begin{equation}
    L = \bigl((x_0, y_0),\; (x_1, y_1),\; \ldots,\; (x_n, y_n)\bigr),
    \quad (x_0,y_0) = s,\; (x_n,y_n) = g,
\end{equation}
where consecutive cells differ by at most~1 in each coordinate and
$|L| = \max(|x_g - x_s|,\; |y_g - y_s|) + 1$.
The Bresenham line is computed in $O(|L|)$ time and serves as a straight-line estimate of the path direction.
It is \emph{not} required to be obstacle-free; obstacles along the line are handled by the corridor expansion mechanism.

\subsection{Stage 2: Per-Point Local Density Estimation}
\label{sec:density}

For each reference-line cell $p \in L$, AILS computes the local obstacle density inside a square window of half-size~$\omega$ (full side length $2\omega + 1$) centred at~$p$:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{W}(p) = \{v_{x,y} \in V : |x - p_x| \leq \omega,\; |y - p_y| \leq \omega\},
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    \sigma(p) = \frac{|\mathcal{O} \cap \mathcal{W}(p)|}{|\mathcal{W}(p)|} \;\in\; [0,\,1].
    \label{eq:density}
\end{equation}
A value $\sigma(p) = 0$ means the window is obstacle-free; $\sigma(p) = 1$ means every cell is blocked.

\subsubsection{Efficient computation via integral images}

An integral image (summed-area table)
\begin{equation}
    I(x, y) = \sum_{x' \leq x,\; y' \leq y} \mathrm{occ}(v_{x',y'})
\end{equation}
is precomputed once in $O(|V|)$ time.
Each window query then costs $O(1)$:
\begin{equation}
    \sigma(p) = \frac{I(x{+}\omega,\, y{+}\omega) - I(x{-}\omega{-}1,\, y{+}\omega)
                     - I(x{+}\omega,\, y{-}\omega{-}1) + I(x{-}\omega{-}1,\, y{-}\omega{-}1)}
                    {(2\omega+1)^{2}}.
\end{equation}
Boundary cases (windows extending outside the grid) are handled by clamping indices.

\subsubsection{Gradient-based lookahead (optional)}

In environments with rapid density transitions, AILS can optionally compute a finite-difference density gradient
\begin{equation}
    \nabla\sigma(p) = \left(\frac{\sigma(p_x{+}1,\,p_y) - \sigma(p_x{-}1,\,p_y)}{2},\;\;
                             \frac{\sigma(p_x,\,p_y{+}1) - \sigma(p_x,\,p_y{-}1)}{2}\right)
\end{equation}
at each reference-line cell.
The gradient magnitude $|\nabla\sigma(p)|$ measures how quickly density is increasing, enabling the corridor to widen \emph{before} a dense region is reached (see the gradient-enhanced radius in Eq.~\ref{eq:radius_grad}).

\subsection{Stage 3: Adaptive Corridor Construction}
\label{sec:corridor}

\subsubsection{Per-point radius}

The corridor radius at each reference-line cell~$p$ is
\begin{equation}
    r(p) = r_{\min} + \bigl\lfloor(r_{\max} - r_{\min}) \cdot \sigma(p)^{\alpha}\bigr\rfloor,
    \label{eq:radius}
\end{equation}
where the three parameters have the following roles:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $r_{\min} \geq 1$: minimum radius, applied in obstacle-free regions (default: 2 cells);
    \item $r_{\max}$: maximum radius, applied when the window is fully blocked (default: $\lceil 0.1 \cdot \min(H, W_g) \rceil$);
    \item $\alpha > 0$: density-sensitivity exponent (default: 1.0). Values $\alpha < 1$ widen the corridor at low densities; $\alpha > 1$ keep it narrow until density is high.
\end{itemize}

\noindent
When $\sigma(p)=0$, $r(p) = r_{\min}$ (narrowest band). When $\sigma(p)=1$, $r(p) = r_{\max}$ (widest band).

\subsubsection{Gradient-enhanced radius (predictive strategy)}
\label{sec:grad_radius}

When gradient-based lookahead is enabled, the radius incorporates the density gradient magnitude:
\begin{equation}
    r(p) = r_{\min} + \bigl\lfloor(r_{\max} - r_{\min}) \cdot \bigl(\sigma(p) + \beta\,|\nabla\sigma(p)|\bigr)^{\alpha}\bigr\rfloor,
    \label{eq:radius_grad}
\end{equation}
where $\beta \geq 0$ controls gradient sensitivity (default: 0.3).
The argument is clamped to $[0, 1]$ before exponentiation.
This allows the corridor to widen \emph{before} the search reaches a dense region, reducing the likelihood of a failed constrained search.

\subsubsection{Corridor cell set}

The corridor is the union of Chebyshev balls centred at each reference-line cell:
\begin{equation}
    C_a = \bigcup_{p \in L} B(p,\, r(p)),
    \qquad
    B(p, r) = \{v \in V : \max(|v_x - p_x|,\; |v_y - p_y|) \leq r\}.
\end{equation}
Only traversable cells ($\mathrm{occ}(v)=0$) that lie within grid bounds are added to~$C_a$.
Algorithm~\ref{alg:corridor} gives the pseudocode; the corridor is stored as a hash set for $O(1)$ membership queries.

\begin{algorithm}[h]
\caption{BuildAdaptiveCorridor}
\label{alg:corridor}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\STATE \textbf{Input:} Grid $G=(V,E,w)$; Bresenham line $L$; parameters $r_{\min}$, $r_{\max}$, $\alpha$, $\omega$
\STATE \textbf{Output:} Corridor set $C_a \subseteq V$ (traversable cells only)
\STATE \textbf{Precondition:} Integral image $I$ has been precomputed from $G$
\STATE
\STATE $C_a \leftarrow \emptyset$
\FOR{$i = 0$ \TO $|L|-1$}
    \STATE $p \leftarrow L[i]$
    \STATE $\sigma(p) \leftarrow$ \textsc{DensityQuery}($I$, $p$, $\omega$) \COMMENT{$O(1)$ via Eq.~\ref{eq:density}}
    \STATE $r(p) \leftarrow r_{\min} + \lfloor(r_{\max} - r_{\min}) \cdot \sigma(p)^{\alpha}\rfloor$ \COMMENT{Eq.~\ref{eq:radius}}
    \FOR{$dx = -r(p)$ \TO $r(p)$}
        \FOR{$dy = -r(p)$ \TO $r(p)$}
            \STATE $v \leftarrow (p_x + dx,\; p_y + dy)$
            \IF{$v$ is within grid bounds \AND $\mathrm{occ}(v) = 0$}
                \STATE $C_a \leftarrow C_a \cup \{v\}$
            \ENDIF
        \ENDFOR
    \ENDFOR
\ENDFOR
\RETURN $C_a$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsubsection{Corridor size bounds}

\begin{theorem}[Worst-case corridor size]\label{thm:corridor_bound}
For a reference line of length~$n = |L|$ and a \emph{uniform} density~$\sigma$ along the entire line, the corridor contains at most $n \cdot (2r(\sigma) + 1)^{2}$ cells, since each of the~$n$ balls contributes at most $(2r(\sigma)+1)^{2}$ cells.
\end{theorem}

\noindent
When density varies along the line, the total is bounded by $\sum_{p\in L}(2r(p)+1)^{2}$.
Because $r(p) = r_{\min}$ wherever $\sigma(p) = 0$, AILS produces substantially smaller corridors than a fixed-$r_{\max}$ band in environments where obstacle-free regions dominate---a property confirmed empirically in Section~\ref{sec:results}.

\subsection{Stage 4: Corridor-Constrained Search with Fallback}
\label{sec:fallback}

\subsubsection{Constrained search}

Any graph-search algorithm~$\mathcal{A}$ (A*, Dijkstra, BFS, etc.)\ is executed on~$G$ with a single modification: the neighbour-expansion function is filtered by occupancy and corridor membership.
For each vertex~$v$ being expanded, the constrained neighbour set is
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{neighbors}_{C}(v) = \{u \in \mathrm{neighbors}(v) : \mathrm{occ}(u) = 0 \;\wedge\; u \in C_a\}.
    \label{eq:neighbor_filter}
\end{equation}
That is, a neighbour~$u$ of~$v$ is considered only if it is (i)~traversable ($\mathrm{occ}(u)=0$) \emph{and} (ii)~inside the corridor ($u \in C_a$).
The occupancy check precedes the corridor-membership test in the implementation, but since $C_a$ contains only traversable cells by construction (Algorithm~\ref{alg:corridor}, line~12), the occupancy check is redundant when the corridor is correctly built; we include it in the definition for formal completeness.
Because $C_a$ is a hash set, the combined check adds $O(1)$ per neighbour.
No other part of the base algorithm is changed; in particular, the heuristic, open-list, and termination condition are identical to the unconstrained version.
Within the corridor, the base algorithm retains its original guarantees: if~$\mathcal{A}$ is optimal on the full grid, it returns the shortest path \emph{that lies entirely inside}~$C_a$.

\subsubsection{Fallback expansion}

If the constrained search finds no path (the open list is exhausted within~$C_a$ without reaching~$g$), AILS expands the corridor by adding all traversable cells within Chebyshev distance~$\Delta r$ of the current corridor boundary:
\begin{equation}
    C_a' = C_a \cup \{v \in V \setminus C_a : \mathrm{occ}(v)=0,\;
           \exists u \in C_a \text{ with } \|v - u\|_{\infty} \leq \Delta r\}.
\end{equation}
This expansion is implemented via a breadth-first traversal from the boundary of~$C_a$.
The constrained search is then re-run on the enlarged corridor.
The process repeats until a path is found or $C_a = V$ (the full grid has been explored).

\begin{theorem}[Completeness]\label{thm:completeness}
Assume the base algorithm~$\mathcal{A}$ is complete on finite graphs (e.g., A* with a consistent heuristic, Dijkstra, or BFS).
If a path from~$s$ to~$g$ exists in~$G$, AILS returns one after at most $\lceil D_{\max}/\Delta r \rceil$ expansion iterations, where $D_{\max}$ is the maximum Chebyshev distance from any cell on \emph{any} $s$-$g$ path to the Bresenham line~$L$.
In the worst case, the corridor grows to encompass all reachable vertices after $O(\max(H, W_g) / \Delta r)$ iterations, reducing to an unconstrained search.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
We prove completeness by \textbf{induction on the expansion index}~$k$.
Define the set of \emph{reachable traversable vertices} $V_{\mathrm{reach}} = \{v \in V : \mathrm{occ}(v)=0 \text{ and } v \text{ is connected to } s \text{ via traversable cells}\}$, and the sequence of corridors $\{C_a^{(k)}\}_{k \geq 0}$:
\begin{align}
    C_a^{(0)} &= \{v \in V : \mathrm{occ}(v)=0,\; \exists\, p \in L \text{ with } \|v-p\|_{\infty} \leq r(p)\}, \label{eq:corridor_base} \\
    C_a^{(k+1)} &= C_a^{(k)} \;\cup\; \{v \in V \setminus C_a^{(k)} : \mathrm{occ}(v)=0,\; \exists\, u \in C_a^{(k)} \text{ with } \|v-u\|_{\infty} \leq \Delta r\}. \label{eq:corridor_inductive}
\end{align}
Note that $C_a^{(k)} \subseteq V_{\mathrm{reach}} \cup (V \setminus V_{\mathrm{reach}})$ but in practice only traversable cells are added; the key property is that $C_a^{(k)} \subseteq \{v \in V : \mathrm{occ}(v) = 0\}$ for all~$k$.

\noindent\textbf{Step 1 (Strict monotonicity).}
By construction, $C_a^{(k)} \subseteq C_a^{(k+1)}$ for all $k \geq 0$.
We show the inclusion is \emph{strict} whenever expansion is triggered.
Suppose the constrained search on~$C_a^{(k)}$ finds no $s$-$g$ path, yet an $s$-$g$ path exists in~$G$.
Then $g \notin C_a^{(k)}$ or $g$ is unreachable within~$C_a^{(k)}$.
Since $g \in V_{\mathrm{reach}}$, there exists a path $\pi = (s = u_0, u_1, \ldots, u_m = g)$ in~$G$ with every $u_i$ traversable.
Let $j$ be the smallest index such that $u_j \notin C_a^{(k)}$; then $u_{j-1} \in C_a^{(k)}$ and $\|u_j - u_{j-1}\|_{\infty} \leq 1 \leq \Delta r$, so $u_j \in C_a^{(k+1)} \setminus C_a^{(k)}$.
Hence $|C_a^{(k+1)}| > |C_a^{(k)}|$ whenever expansion is triggered.

\noindent\textbf{Step 2 (Inductive coverage growth).}
We prove by induction on~$k$ that every traversable cell within Chebyshev distance $r_{\max} + k \cdot \Delta r$ of the reference line~$L$ is contained in~$C_a^{(k)}$.

\emph{Base case ($k=0$):}
By Eq.~\eqref{eq:corridor_base}, every traversable cell~$v$ satisfying $\|v - p\|_{\infty} \leq r(p)$ for some $p \in L$ is in~$C_a^{(0)}$.
Since $r(p) \leq r_{\max}$ for all~$p$, this includes every traversable cell within Chebyshev distance~$r_{\max}$ of~$L$.

\emph{Inductive step ($k \to k{+}1$):}
Assume every traversable cell~$v$ with $\min_{p \in L}\|v-p\|_{\infty} \leq r_{\max} + k \cdot \Delta r$ belongs to~$C_a^{(k)}$.
Let $v'$ be any traversable cell with $\min_{p \in L}\|v'-p\|_{\infty} \leq r_{\max} + (k{+}1)\Delta r$.
Then there exists a cell~$u$ on the Chebyshev-geodesic from~$v'$ towards~$L$ satisfying $\|v' - u\|_{\infty} \leq \Delta r$ and $\min_{p \in L}\|u-p\|_{\infty} \leq r_{\max} + k \cdot \Delta r$.
By the inductive hypothesis $u \in C_a^{(k)}$, so Eq.~\eqref{eq:corridor_inductive} yields $v' \in C_a^{(k+1)}$.

\noindent\textbf{Step 3 (Finite termination).}
The grid~$V$ is finite ($|V| = H \times W_g$), so $V_{\mathrm{reach}}$ is finite.
By Step~1, $|C_a^{(k)}|$ strictly increases at each expansion.
Since $|C_a^{(k)}| \leq |V_{\mathrm{reach}}|$ for all~$k$, the expansion loop can execute at most $|V_{\mathrm{reach}}| - |C_a^{(0)}|$ times.
More tightly, Step~2 shows that after $K = \lceil (\max(H, W_g) - r_{\min}) / \Delta r \rceil$ iterations, every traversable cell in the grid lies within $C_a^{(K)}$; in particular, $V_{\mathrm{reach}} \subseteq C_a^{(K)}$.

\noindent\textbf{Step 4 (Completeness).}
At some iteration $k^{*} \leq K$, the corridor satisfies $V_{\mathrm{reach}} \subseteq C_a^{(k^{*})}$.
The constrained search on~$C_a^{(k^{*})}$ is then equivalent to an unconstrained search on the reachable subgraph of~$G$.
Because~$\mathcal{A}$ is complete on finite graphs by assumption, it finds a path whenever one exists.
Therefore, AILS is complete.

More precisely, if $\pi^{*}$ is any $s$-$g$ path in~$G$ and $D_{\max} = \max_{v \in \pi^{*}} \min_{p \in L}\|v-p\|_{\infty}$, then setting $k^{*} = \lceil (D_{\max} - r_{\min}) / \Delta r \rceil$ guarantees $\pi^{*} \subseteq C_a^{(k^{*})}$, so AILS finds a path (not necessarily~$\pi^{*}$ itself) by iteration~$k^{*}$ at the latest.
\end{proof}

\noindent
\textbf{Remark.}
The fallback is a safety mechanism, not the primary source of AILS's efficiency.
In our experiments (Section~\ref{sec:results}), the initial adaptive corridor was sufficient in the vast majority of cases; fallback expansion was triggered in fewer than 2\% of test instances at 25\% obstacle density.

\subsection{Complete Algorithm}

Algorithm~\ref{alg:ails} combines the four stages into a single procedure.
All parameters have defaults stated in Sections~\ref{sec:density}--\ref{sec:corridor}; the only required inputs are the grid, start, goal, and base search algorithm.

\begin{algorithm}[h]
\caption{AILS-Enhanced Pathfinding}
\label{alg:ails}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\STATE \textbf{Input:} Grid $G=(V,E,w)$; start $s$; goal $g$; base algorithm $\mathcal{A}$
\STATE \textbf{Output:} Shortest path $\pi$ within corridor, or $\emptyset$ if no $s$-$g$ path exists in~$G$
\STATE \textbf{Parameters:} $r_{\min}$, $r_{\max}$, $\alpha$, $\omega$, $\Delta r$ (all with defaults; see text)
\STATE
\STATE $I \leftarrow$ \textsc{BuildIntegralImage}($G$) \COMMENT{$O(|V|)$ preprocessing}
\STATE $L \leftarrow$ \textsc{BresenhamLine}($s$, $g$) \COMMENT{Reference line, $O(|L|)$}
\STATE $C_a \leftarrow$ \textsc{BuildAdaptiveCorridor}($G$, $L$, $I$, $r_{\min}$, $r_{\max}$, $\alpha$, $\omega$)
\STATE
\REPEAT
    \STATE $\pi \leftarrow \mathcal{A}$.\textsc{Search}($G$, $s$, $g$, $C_a$) \COMMENT{Constrained search (Eq.~\ref{eq:neighbor_filter})}
    \IF{$\pi \neq \emptyset$}
        \RETURN $\pi$
    \ENDIF
    \IF{$C_a = V$}
        \RETURN $\emptyset$ \COMMENT{Full grid explored; no path exists}
    \ENDIF
    \STATE $C_a \leftarrow$ \textsc{ExpandCorridor}($C_a$, $\Delta r$) \COMMENT{BFS boundary expansion}
\UNTIL{false}
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Multi-Strategy Selection}
\label{sec:strategy}

AILS provides three corridor strategies, selected by a lightweight scan of the Bresenham line at initialisation:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Base (fixed-width).}
    If $\sigma(p) = 0$ for every $p \in L$ (i.e., the Bresenham line is obstacle-free), a fixed corridor of radius~$r_{\min}$ suffices.
    This avoids the per-point density computation entirely.

    \item \textbf{Standard (density-adaptive).}
    If obstacles are detected along~$L$ but the density gradient is small (max $|\nabla\sigma| < 0.1$), Eq.~\ref{eq:radius} is used.
    This is the default and covers the majority of scenarios.

    \item \textbf{Predictive (gradient-enhanced).}
    If the density changes rapidly along~$L$ (max $|\nabla\sigma| \geq 0.1$), Eq.~\ref{eq:radius_grad} is used, widening the corridor \emph{before} dense regions are reached.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent
The threshold $|\nabla\sigma| = 0.1$ is a tunable hyper-parameter; in our experiments the default value worked well across all tested patterns (Section~\ref{sec:ablation}).

\subsection{Complexity Analysis}
\label{sec:complexity}

\textbf{Role of $r_{\max}$ and the linear-cost regime.}
Throughout this analysis, $r_{\max}$ is a \emph{constant hyperparameter} fixed before the search begins.
In a production deployment, $r_{\max}$ is set to an absolute constant (e.g., $r_{\max} = 30$ or $50$~cells) independent of the grid dimension~$N$, reflecting the maximum detour range relevant to the application.
The default formula $r_{\max} = \lceil 0.1 \cdot \min(H, W_g) \rceil$ is provided only as a convenience for parameter-free experimentation; it is not intended to scale indefinitely.
The key observation is that, for any \emph{fixed} $r_{\max}$, the corridor construction cost is \emph{strictly linear} in the grid dimension and is therefore amortised by the quadratic-logarithmic search cost it displaces, as shown below.

\begin{theorem}[Time complexity]\label{thm:time}
Let the base algorithm~$\mathcal{A}$ have worst-case time $O(f(n))$ on a graph with~$n$ vertices.
The total time of one AILS invocation (without fallback) is
\begin{equation}
    O\!\bigl(\underbrace{|V|}_{\text{integral image}} +
             \underbrace{|L| \cdot r_{\max}^{2}}_{\text{corridor construction}} +
             \underbrace{f(|C_a|)}_{\text{constrained search}}\bigr).
\end{equation}
Each fallback expansion adds $O(|C_a|)$ for the BFS boundary traversal plus $O(f(|C_a'|))$ for the re-search.
With~$k$ fallback rounds the total becomes $O(|V| + |L|\cdot r_{\max}^{2} + \sum_{i=0}^{k} f(|C_a^{(i)}|))$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The integral image is built in $O(|V|)$.
The Bresenham line has $|L| = O(\max(H, W_g))$ cells; for each, the density query is $O(1)$ (from the integral image) and the ball enumeration visits $(2r(p)+1)^{2} \leq (2r_{\max}+1)^{2}$ cells, giving corridor construction cost $O(|L| \cdot r_{\max}^{2})$.
The constrained search runs on $|C_a|$ nodes.
\end{proof}

\noindent
\textbf{Linear-cost guarantee and amortisation for fixed $r_{\max}$.}
On an $N{\times}N$ grid, $|V| = N^{2}$, $|L| = O(N)$, and $r_{\max}$ is a fixed constant.
The corridor construction cost is therefore $O(N \cdot r_{\max}^{2}) = O(N)$, which is strictly dominated by the $O(N^{2})$ integral-image preprocessing.
Crucially, this $O(N)$ overhead displaces an unconstrained A* search that costs $O(|V| \log |V|) = O(N^{2} \log N)$ in the worst case.
Since the corridor typically contains only a fraction $\rho = |C_a|/|V|$ of the grid (empirically $\rho \in [0.02, 0.10]$), the constrained search costs $O(\rho \cdot N^{2} \log(\rho \cdot N^{2}))$, and the \emph{net} saving is $O((1 - \rho) \cdot N^{2} \log N)$---quadratic-logarithmic---which asymptotically dominates the linear corridor-construction overhead.
In other words, the $O(N)$ cost of building the corridor is \emph{amortised} by the $O(N^{2} \log N)$ saving in the search phase.

Even under the default scaling rule $r_{\max} = 0.1\,N$ (used only in our experiments for convenience), the construction cost becomes $O(N \cdot (0.1\,N)^{2}) = O(0.01\,N^{3})$.
In practice this cubic term is negligible for the grid sizes we test ($N \leq 500$), because the constant factor $0.01$ is small and the corridor construction loop terminates early for low-density cells (where $r(p) \ll r_{\max}$).
Nevertheless, for production deployments on very large grids ($N > 10^{3}$), $r_{\max}$ \textbf{must} be fixed as an absolute constant (e.g., $r_{\max} = 50$) to guarantee the linear corridor-construction regime.

\begin{theorem}[Space complexity]
AILS uses $O(|V| + |C_a|)$ additional memory: $O(|V|)$ for the integral image and $O(|C_a|)$ for the corridor hash set and the data structures of~$\mathcal{A}$.
\end{theorem}

\noindent
In our experiments, $|C_a|$ is typically 2--10\% of $|V|$ (see corridor-efficiency data in Section~\ref{sec:results}), so the constrained-search term dominates the integral-image term only on small grids.

\subsection{Correctness Properties}
\label{sec:correctness}

The following properties hold under the assumption that the base algorithm~$\mathcal{A}$ is \emph{complete} and \emph{optimal} on finite graphs (e.g., A* with a consistent heuristic, Dijkstra, or BFS on unit-cost graphs).

\begin{theorem}[Optimality within corridor]
If the adaptive corridor~$C_a$ covers at least one globally optimal path~$\pi^{*}$, then the constrained search returns~$\pi^{*}$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The constrained search runs~$\mathcal{A}$ on the subgraph induced by~$C_a$.
Because~$\mathcal{A}$ is optimal on finite graphs and~$\pi^{*} \subseteq C_a$, no shorter path within~$C_a$ exists, so the returned path equals~$\pi^{*}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Convergence and termination]
The fallback loop (Algorithm~\ref{alg:ails}) terminates after finitely many iterations.
$C_a$ grows monotonically ($C_a^{(k)} \subsetneq C_a^{(k+1)}$) whenever expansion is triggered, so the process terminates when either a path is found or $C_a = V$.
\end{theorem}

\noindent
\textbf{Note on bounded sub-optimality.}
Before any fallback expansion, the corridor may not cover a globally optimal path; in that case the returned path is optimal \emph{within the corridor} but may be sub-optimal globally.
We do not claim a formal $(1+\epsilon)$ bound on the sub-optimality ratio because the gap depends on the obstacle configuration and the corridor parameters.
Empirically, on the grid sizes and densities we tested, paths returned without fallback are within 1--3\% of the global optimum (see the optimality-rate columns in Section~\ref{sec:ablation}).

\subsubsection{Theoretical discussion of the optimality gap}
\label{sec:optimality_gap}

A natural question is whether AILS admits a universal $(1{+}\epsilon)$ sub-optimality bound analogous to those available for weighted A*~\cite{hart1968formal} or bounded-suboptimal MAPF solvers such as EECBS~\cite{li2021eecbs}.
We argue that such a bound is inherently difficult for corridor-based methods on non-uniform grids, for the following reasons.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Instance dependence of the gap.}
    The sub-optimality of the corridor-constrained path $\pi^{*}_{C}$ relative to the global optimum $\pi^{*}$ depends on whether $\pi^{*}$ lies inside the corridor~$C_a$.
    This, in turn, depends on the specific obstacle configuration: an adversarially placed obstacle cluster just outside the corridor can force $\pi^{*}$ to follow a detour that $C_a$ does not cover, while a minor perturbation of the same configuration may place $\pi^{*}$ entirely within~$C_a$.
    A worst-case $(1{+}\epsilon)$ bound would therefore need to quantify the maximum possible detour cost as a function of the corridor parameters \emph{and} the obstacle geometry, which is at least as hard as characterising the topology of all reachable paths in the grid.

    \item \textbf{Non-convexity of the corridor.}
    Because the AILS corridor is a union of Chebyshev balls of varying radii, it is in general a non-convex subset of the grid.
    Classical sub-optimality analyses for corridor methods (e.g., those based on convex safe-flight corridors~\cite{chen2024bubble}) rely on convexity to bound path deviations.
    The non-convex shape of the AILS corridor, while beneficial for reducing corridor area, precludes direct application of these analyses.

    \item \textbf{Fallback restores optimality in the limit.}
    The fallback expansion mechanism (Theorem~\ref{thm:completeness}) guarantees that the corridor eventually covers all reachable cells, at which point the search is equivalent to unconstrained A* and returns the globally optimal path.
    In this sense, AILS provides an \emph{anytime} guarantee: the solution quality is monotonically non-decreasing with each expansion round, and global optimality is achieved in the worst case.
    However, no constant~$\epsilon$ can bound the gap \emph{before} expansion, as this gap is zero on some instances and arbitrarily close to the ratio $\mathrm{cost}(\text{longest feasible path}) / \mathrm{cost}(\pi^{*})$ on worst-case instances.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent
We regard the derivation of \emph{instance-class-specific} sub-optimality bounds---e.g., for random obstacle fields with bounded density---as an open theoretical problem and a direction for future work.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Synthetic Dataset: A Controlled Exploration of Obstacle Topology}
\label{sec:dataset}

The experimental design is a \emph{controlled exploration of obstacle topology}: we systematically vary grid size, obstacle density, and obstacle pattern to isolate their individual and combined effects on corridor-based search.
Synthetic grids are used deliberately, because they allow precise control over these variables---something that real-world maps, with their fixed and correlated obstacle distributions, do not permit.
This controlled setting is necessary for the parameter-sensitivity analysis in Section~\ref{sec:ablation} and for identifying the topological regimes where AILS is most and least effective.

We generate grid maps spanning eight sizes ($50{\times}50$, $100{\times}100$, $150{\times}150$, $200{\times}200$, $250{\times}250$, $300{\times}300$, $400{\times}400$, $500{\times}500$), seven obstacle densities (10\%--40\% in 5\% steps), and five obstacle patterns:
\emph{Random} (uniform i.i.d.\ placement),
\emph{Clustered} (Gaussian-centred obstacle groups),
\emph{Maze} (corridors and walls generated via recursive division),
\emph{Room} (rectangular rooms connected by doorways), and
\emph{Open} (sparse, low-density placement).
For each configuration, 50--200 random start--goal pairs are sampled; pairs for which no path exists are discarded before timing begins, so that every measured instance has a valid solution.

\subsection{Baseline Algorithms}
\label{sec:baselines}

We select baselines to cover the main classes of uninformed and informed search:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{A*}~\cite{hart1968formal} with the octile-distance heuristic (admissible and consistent on 8-connected grids).  This is the standard optimal planner against which corridor overhead is measured.
    \item \textbf{Dijkstra}~\cite{dijkstra1959note}: an uninformed optimal baseline that establishes the upper bound on node expansion.
    \item \textbf{BFS}: optimal on unit-cost grids; included to separate the effect of heuristic guidance from search-space restriction.
    \item \textbf{Bidirectional A*}~\cite{russell2016artificial}: explores from both endpoints, halving the effective search depth.
\end{itemize}
JPS~\cite{harabor2011online} is excluded from the medium-scale experiments (Section~\ref{sec:experiments}) because it applies only to uniform-cost grids, whereas our medium-scale evaluation uses 8-connected grids with cardinal cost~1 and diagonal cost~$\sqrt{2}$ (non-uniform).
Hierarchical methods (HPA*, CH) require preprocessing incompatible with our single-query, no-preprocessing setting.
For the large-scale benchmark (Section~\ref{sec:large_scale}), we include JPS as an additional baseline, using a uniform-cost 4-connected grid configuration to enable a direct comparison with the leading search-space reduction technique for large grids.

\subsection{AILS Variants}

We test two AILS configurations:
\textbf{AILS-Base} (fixed-width corridor, $r(p) = r_{\min}$ for all~$p$, corresponding to the base strategy of Section~\ref{sec:strategy}) and
\textbf{AILS-Adaptive} (density-adaptive corridor, Eq.~\ref{eq:radius}).
Both use A* as the internal search algorithm.
Default parameters are $r_{\min}=2$, $r_{\max} = \lceil 0.1 \cdot \min(H, W_g)\rceil$, $\alpha = 1.0$, $\omega = 3$ (window half-size, so the full window is $7 \times 7$), and $\Delta r = 2$.
Parameter sensitivity is studied in Section~\ref{sec:ablation}.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Execution time}: wall-clock time in milliseconds (median of three runs per instance).
    \item \textbf{Visited nodes}: number of nodes expanded (popped from the open list).
    \item \textbf{Path cost}: total edge weight of the returned path.
    \item \textbf{Optimality rate}: fraction of instances where the returned path cost equals the A* optimal cost.
    \item \textbf{Corridor efficiency}: $|C_a| / |V|$, the fraction of the grid inside the corridor.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Statistical Methodology}

Pairwise comparisons use two-sided paired $t$-tests (one per start--goal pair) with significance level $\alpha_{\text{stat}} = 0.05$.
Effect sizes are reported as Cohen's $d$; we follow the standard thresholds ($|d| < 0.2$: negligible; $0.2$--$0.5$: small; $0.5$--$0.8$: medium; $\geq 0.8$: large).
Normality of paired differences is checked with Shapiro--Wilk tests; for multi-group comparisons we use one-way ANOVA with Tukey HSD post-hoc correction.

\subsection{Hardware and Software}

All experiments ran on an Intel Core i7-12700K (3.6\,GHz, 12 cores) with 64\,GB DDR5 RAM, Ubuntu~22.04 LTS.
The implementation is in Python~3.10 using NumPy and SciPy; each configuration is evaluated on 100 random start--goal pairs.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Summary of Findings}

The experimental results support four main observations.

\paragraph{Node reduction is consistent and statistically significant.}
Across $200{\times}200$ grids at 25\% density, AILS-Base reduces expanded nodes by 56.0\% ($d = 0.82$) and AILS-Adaptive by 51.4\% ($d = 0.76$), both at $p < 0.001$.
However, node reduction does not automatically translate to wall-clock speedup: corridor construction adds fixed overhead that must be amortised by the search-space savings.

\paragraph{Time competitiveness requires sufficiently large grids.}
On grids below ${\approx}300{\times}300$, the corridor overhead exceeds the search savings; above this crossover, AILS-Adaptive matches or beats A* in execution time.
The crossover point is implementation-dependent (Python in our case) and would shift with a more efficient language.

\paragraph{Performance is environment-dependent.}
AILS is most effective on random and open obstacle patterns at 10--25\% density.
Clustered, maze, and room patterns---where the optimal path deviates substantially from the Bresenham reference line---degrade performance because repeated corridor expansion is needed.

\paragraph{Efficiency scales dramatically at large grid sizes.}
The large-scale benchmark (Section~\ref{sec:large_scale}) reveals that AILS's advantages intensify as grid size grows.
At $10{,}000{\times}10{,}000$ grids, AILS achieves a $39.9{\times}$ wall-clock speedup over A* and a $30.7{\times}$ speedup over JPS, with 97.2\% node reduction.
Power-law fitting confirms AILS scales as $O(n^{0.51})$---sublinear---compared to $O(n^{0.85})$ for A* and JPS.
The corridor covers only 0.11\% of the grid at this scale, with Cohen's $d = 1.65$ versus A* (a large effect that \emph{increases} with grid size), confirming that the algorithm's efficiency advantage compounds as problem scale grows.

\subsection{Topological Robustness: The Adaptive Value Proposition}
\label{sec:robustness}

A natural question is why one should prefer AILS over the simpler ILS~\cite{elshahed2025ils}, given that ILS achieves a higher raw node-reduction percentage (up to 87\%) on uniform random grids.
The answer lies in \emph{topological robustness}: the ability to maintain search-space efficiency across environments with varying obstacle structure.

ILS applies a single global corridor radius~$r$, which is optimal when obstacle density is spatially uniform---the radius can be set just wide enough to accommodate detours, and every segment of the corridor is equally useful.
However, when density varies along the reference line (as in mazes, room layouts, or clustered obstacles), the global radius must accommodate the \emph{densest} segment.
Every open segment of the corridor is then over-expanded to width~$r_{\max}$, adding cells that the search will never need.
Table~\ref{tab:pattern_analysis} confirms this: on Maze and Room patterns, where density varies sharply along the reference line, a fixed-width corridor provides almost no node reduction (0.7\% for Maze, 0.3\% for Room) and incurs heavy fallback-expansion overhead.

AILS addresses this by computing a per-cell radius $r(p) = r_{\min} + \lfloor (r_{\max} - r_{\min}) \cdot \sigma(p)^{\alpha} \rfloor$ via $O(1)$ integral-image queries.
The resulting corridor is \emph{non-convex}: it can thread through narrow passages at width~$r_{\min}$ and expand only where local density demands.
This means the corridor area scales with the \emph{weighted average} density along the reference line, not the peak density---a qualitative advantage over any fixed-width scheme when the environment is heterogeneous.
We therefore position AILS not as a universally faster successor to ILS, but as a \textbf{topologically robust} framework suited to environments where obstacle distribution is non-uniform, the typical case in real-world robotics and game maps.

\subsection{Analysis of the Adaptive Mechanism}

\subsubsection{Why per-cell adaptation helps}
Fixed-width corridors waste cells in open regions and may be too narrow near obstacles.
By computing density at each reference-line cell, AILS allocates corridor width where it is needed, keeping the total corridor area proportional to the \emph{weighted average} radius rather than the maximum:
\begin{equation}
    |C_a| \;\leq\; \sum_{p \in L} (2r(p)+1)^{2}
    \;\ll\; |L| \cdot (2r_{\max}+1)^{2}
\end{equation}
whenever most of the reference line passes through low-density regions.

\subsubsection{Parameter sensitivity}
Table~\ref{tab:ablation_alpha} shows that $\alpha = 1.0$ gives the best trade-off between corridor size and optimality rate on our benchmark; $\alpha < 1$ over-expands, and $\alpha > 1$ produces corridors that are too narrow in moderately dense regions.
The window half-size~$\omega$ exhibits a bias--variance trade-off (Table~\ref{tab:ablation_window}): small windows ($2\omega{+}1 = 3$) are sensitive to local noise; large windows ($2\omega{+}1 \geq 9$) smooth away density transitions.
The range $2\omega{+}1 \in [5, 7]$ balances precision and robustness.
Automatic tuning of these parameters (e.g., via Bayesian optimisation over a training set of maps) is a direction for future work.

\subsection{Positioning Relative to Prior Methods}

Table~\ref{tab:comparison} in Section~\ref{sec:related} gives an overview; here we add qualitative notes.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{JPS}~\cite{harabor2011online} achieves 10--100$\times$ speedups on uniform-cost grids by pruning symmetric paths.
    AILS applies to any weighted grid but achieves more modest speedups.
    The two ideas are compatible: JPS could be used as the base algorithm inside an AILS corridor on uniform grids, although we have not tested this combination.

    \item \textbf{HPA*}~\cite{botea2004near} and \textbf{Contraction Hierarchies}~\cite{geisberger2008contraction} achieve fast queries after expensive preprocessing.
    AILS has zero preprocessing, making it suitable for single-query or dynamic settings where the map may change between queries.

    \item \textbf{D*~Lite}~\cite{koenig2002d} / \textbf{LPA*}~\cite{koenig2004lifelong} incrementally update a search tree when the map changes between queries.
    AILS restricts the search space \emph{within} a single query; the two approaches are complementary (see Section~\ref{sec:related}).

    \item \textbf{ILS}~\cite{elshahed2025ils} (our earlier work) uses a uniform corridor radius along the Bresenham line.
    AILS extends ILS with per-point density estimation, non-convex corridor shapes, automatic strategy selection, and local (rather than global) fallback expansion.
    Table~\ref{tab:ils_vs_ails} in Section~\ref{sec:ils_novelty} gives a detailed comparison.

    \item \textbf{Other corridor methods}~\cite{huang2024corridor,cohen2023corridor,wu2023corridor,wang2025adaptive} use fixed or region-level widths and are often coupled to a specific planner.
    AILS adapts at the per-cell level and is algorithm-agnostic.
    However, we note that some of these methods address multi-agent coordination, which AILS does not.

    \item \textbf{MCPP-GAK}~\cite{lee2025mcpp} solves multi-agent coverage, a different problem.
    In principle, AILS could accelerate the internal single-agent pathfinding calls within a MCPP framework, but we have not evaluated this integration.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Limitations}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Overhead on small grids.}
    For grids smaller than ${\approx}100{\times}100$, AILS is slower than A* in wall-clock time.
    We recommend it only when the grid dimension exceeds this threshold (or when node count, rather than time, is the binding constraint).

    \item \textbf{High-density and structured environments.}
    At densities above 30\% or in maze/room patterns, corridor expansion dominates.
    AILS's density model assumes obstacles are spatially distributed along the reference line; when the optimal path must navigate through narrow doorways or around large clusters, the Bresenham line is a poor reference and multiple expansions are needed.

    \item \textbf{No formal sub-optimality bound.}
    When the initial corridor does not cover an optimal path, the returned path is optimal \emph{within the corridor} but may be globally sub-optimal.
    Our experiments show paths within 1--3\% of optimal, but we have not proved a worst-case bound.
    As discussed in Section~\ref{sec:optimality_gap}, a universal $(1{+}\epsilon)$ guarantee is inherently difficult for corridor-based methods on non-uniform grids due to the instance dependence of the optimality gap and the non-convexity of the AILS corridor.

    \item \textbf{Parameter dependence.}
    Defaults work well for random/open patterns at 10--25\% density, but different environments may benefit from different $r_{\max}$, $\alpha$, and $\omega$ values.
    No automatic tuning mechanism is provided in the current framework.

    \item \textbf{Memory.}
    The corridor hash set uses $O(|C_a|)$ memory.
    For grids with millions of cells, a bitmap representation (one bit per cell) would reduce this overhead.

    \item \textbf{Synthetic benchmarks as a controlled first stage.}
    The medium-scale experiments (Section~\ref{sec:results}) use procedurally generated grids with five controlled obstacle topologies (Random, Clustered, Maze, Room, Open).
    This is a deliberate methodological choice: the primary goal of those experiments is to characterise \emph{how obstacle topology and density affect corridor-based search}, which requires precise, independent control over these variables.
    The large-scale benchmark (Section~\ref{sec:large_scale}) extends this to massive grids ($10^6$ to $10^8$ cells) with Clustered, Mixed, and Random patterns, confirming that AILS's sublinear scaling persists at practical problem sizes.
    However, neither study evaluates on real-world occupancy maps.
    Evaluation on the \textbf{Moving AI Lab benchmark suite}~\cite{sturtevant2012benchmarks}, which provides thousands of scenarios from commercial video games (Dragon Age, StarCraft, WarCraft~III) and city maps, is an important next step for external validation on maps with heterogeneous, correlated obstacle distributions that may not be well represented by any single synthetic pattern.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Potential Applications}

The characteristics of AILS---no preprocessing, moderate node reduction, algorithm agnosticism---are advantageous for:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Mobile robotics}~\cite{siciliano2016springer,bento2021autonomous}: reduced node expansion supports higher replanning rates on resource-constrained platforms.
    \item \textbf{UAV navigation}~\cite{goerzen2010survey,majeed2024uav,gao2024drone}: the corridor concept extends naturally to 3-D voxel grids, though we have not validated this experimentally.
    \item \textbf{Warehouse automation}~\cite{wu2020warehouse}: regular shelf layouts with low-to-moderate obstacle density match AILS's effective operating regime.
    \item \textbf{Multi-agent systems}~\cite{stern2019mapf}: faster single-agent planning could accelerate inner-loop pathfinding in MAPF solvers, but integration would require further study.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Implementation Notes}

We offer practical guidance for adopting AILS:
store $C_a$ as a hash set (or bitmap for very large grids) for $O(1)$ membership queries;
precompute the integral image once per map and amortise across queries;
start with defaults ($r_{\min}{=}2$, $r_{\max}{=}\lceil 0.1 \min(H,W_g)\rceil$, $\alpha{=}1.0$, $\omega{=}3$, $\Delta r{=}2$) and adjust based on observed corridor-efficiency and optimality-rate metrics.

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}
\label{sec:conclusion}

\subsection{Summary}

We presented AILS, a corridor-based framework for grid pathfinding that constructs a variable-width search band along a Bresenham reference line.
The corridor radius at each reference-line cell is computed from the local obstacle density (via an integral-image query), producing a narrow band in open regions and a wide band near obstacles.
Any admissible graph-search algorithm can be plugged in without modification; a fallback expansion mechanism preserves completeness.

\subsection{Empirical Findings}

On synthetic 8-connected grids ($50{\times}50$ to $500{\times}500$, five obstacle patterns, 10--40\% density), AILS reduces node expansion by 51--56\% relative to A* on $200{\times}200$ grids (Cohen's $d = 0.76$--$0.82$, $p < 0.001$).
Node reduction scales with grid size, reaching 76.8\% on $500{\times}500$.
Wall-clock time competitiveness emerges at ${\approx}300{\times}300$ and above; below this threshold, corridor-construction overhead exceeds the search-space savings in our Python implementation.
AILS performs best on random and open obstacle layouts at 10--25\% density.
Performance degrades in high-density, maze, and room environments where the optimal path deviates substantially from the reference line.

The large-scale benchmark (630 trials on grids from $1{,}000{\times}1{,}000$ to $10{,}000{\times}10{,}000$) confirms and amplifies these findings.
At $10{,}000{\times}10{,}000$, AILS achieves a $39.9{\times}$ wall-clock speedup over A* (97.5\% time reduction, 97.2\% node reduction) with Cohen's $d = 1.65$ (large effect, $p < 10^{-5}$).
AILS also outperforms JPS by $30.7{\times}$, with JPS exhibiting a 67\% timeout rate on the largest grids.
Power-law scaling analysis yields $T_{\text{AILS}} \sim n^{0.51}$ (effectively sublinear) versus $T_{\text{A*}} \sim n^{0.85}$ and $T_{\text{JPS}} \sim n^{0.84}$ (both near-linear), and the corridor covers only 0.11\% of the grid at $10{,}000{\times}10{,}000$---confirming that corridor efficiency improves as grid size grows.

\subsection{Limitations and Open Problems}

We did not establish a formal $(1{+}\epsilon)$ sub-optimality bound for the corridor-constrained path; our near-optimality claim is empirical (Section~\ref{sec:correctness}), and we have shown in Section~\ref{sec:optimality_gap} why a universal bound is inherently difficult for corridor-based methods on non-uniform grids.
The current parameter defaults work well across most tested conditions, but no automatic tuning procedure is provided.
The medium-scale experiments use synthetic grids, which enables controlled exploration of obstacle topology for the parameter-sensitivity analysis (Section~\ref{sec:ablation}) and identification of AILS's effective operating regimes; the large-scale benchmark extends this to grids up to $10{,}000{\times}10{,}000$ with clustered, mixed, and random patterns.
Evaluation on real-world maps such as the Moving AI Lab benchmark suite~\cite{sturtevant2012benchmarks} remains an important future step for validating generalisation to correlated, heterogeneous obstacle distributions found in game and robotics environments.
An ablation isolating the contribution of each AILS component (density estimation, gradient lookahead, fallback expansion) is reported in Section~\ref{sec:ablation}, but a more systematic design-of-experiments study could further clarify interactions among parameters.

\subsection{Future Directions}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Real-world map benchmarks.}  Evaluating AILS on the Moving AI Lab suite~\cite{sturtevant2012benchmarks} (Dragon Age, StarCraft, WarCraft~III maps and city street networks) would validate generalisation to real-world obstacle distributions with correlated structure that differs from our synthetic patterns.
    The large-scale experiments (Section~\ref{sec:large_scale}) already demonstrate competitive performance at $10{,}000{\times}10{,}000$ grids, suggesting the approach is practically applicable; real-world maps would test topological robustness further.
    \item \textbf{AILS $+$ JPS integration.}  Our large-scale experiments show that JPS degrades at scale on irregular grids (67\% timeout at $10{,}000{\times}10{,}000$), while AILS remains efficient.
    On uniform-cost grids where JPS is applicable, using JPS as the base algorithm \emph{inside} an AILS corridor could combine corridor-based search-space restriction with JPS's pruning; this combination has not yet been evaluated.
    \item \textbf{Instance-class sub-optimality bounds.}  Deriving $(1{+}\epsilon)$ guarantees for specific obstacle distributions (e.g., random fields with bounded density) would provide theoretical assurance complementing our empirical near-optimality results.
    \item \textbf{3-D and continuous spaces.}  Extending the corridor concept to voxel grids (UAV navigation) or continuous configuration spaces (manipulators) is a natural next step.
    \item \textbf{Incremental corridor updates.}  Combining AILS with incremental planners (D*~Lite, LPA*) could enable efficient replanning when the map changes between queries.
    \item \textbf{Automatic parameter tuning.}  Learning $r_{\max}$, $\alpha$, and $\omega$ from environment features (e.g., via Bayesian optimisation or a lightweight neural network) would remove the need for manual selection, particularly important for the large-scale setting where parameter sensitivity may differ from medium-scale.
    \item \textbf{Integration with MAPF/CPP.}  Using AILS as the inner planner inside multi-agent frameworks (e.g., CBS, MCPP-GAK) may reduce per-agent planning cost; the $40{\times}$ speedup over A* at large scales suggests meaningful gains in MAPF scenarios with large environments.
    \item \textbf{GPU acceleration.}  Corridor construction and density computation are embarrassingly parallel; a GPU implementation could lower the time-competitiveness crossover to smaller grids and further improve the already favourable large-scale performance.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Concluding Remarks}

AILS is a practical, preprocessing-free technique for reducing the search space of grid-based pathfinding.
Its principal contribution over the simpler ILS~\cite{elshahed2025ils} is not raw speed on uniform-density grids---where ILS's global-radius approach already performs well---but \emph{topological robustness}: the per-cell adaptive corridor maintains efficiency across environments with spatially varying obstacle density, where a fixed-width corridor must over-expand to accommodate the densest segment.
This robustness, combined with algorithm agnosticism and remarkably favourable scalability---$O(n^{0.51})$ sublinear time at large scales, $39.9{\times}$ speedup over A* at $10{,}000{\times}10{,}000$ grids---makes AILS well suited for real-world environments whose obstacle topology is heterogeneous and not known in advance.
These strengths are balanced by clear limitations: overhead on small grids, degradation at high obstacle densities, and the absence of a formal sub-optimality guarantee.
We hope the framework, together with the open-source implementation available at \url{https://github.com/Amr-path/Adaptive-ILS}, provides a useful building block for researchers and practitioners working on large-scale navigation problems.
